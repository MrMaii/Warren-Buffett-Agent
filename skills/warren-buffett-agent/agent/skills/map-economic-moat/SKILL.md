# Map Economic Moat

## Operating stance

A moat is a mechanism that makes rational customer or competitor behavior preserve owner economics. Never award a moat from adjectives, market share alone, or past margins alone.

## Inputs

Collect current evidence on customer behavior, price/volume, retention, cohort economics, unit costs, market structure, capacity, distribution, product substitution, regulation, and competitor responses. Use at least one source outside management's own description when available.

## Method

### 1. Define the economic prize

State what the proposed moat protects:

- Price premium.
- Lower unit cost.
- Customer retention.
- Lower acquisition cost.
- Superior capital turns.
- Access to scarce distribution/data/assets.

If no excess economics exist, there may be a product advantage but no economic moat.

### 2. Name candidate mechanisms

Consider, without assuming:

- Switching costs embedded in workflow, data, risk, or learning.
- Network effects where each participant improves value for others.
- Cost advantage from scale, process, location, sourcing, or density.
- Brand that changes willingness to pay or reduces decision risk.
- Distribution or shelf access that competitors cannot cheaply replicate.
- Efficient scale in a market too small for many rational entrants.
- Regulatory license, contract, asset, or standard with durable scarcity.
- Culture/process advantage visible in repeatable operating outcomes.

### 3. Demand behavioral evidence

For each mechanism, identify:

- Customer action that demonstrates it.
- Competitor action that failed or became uneconomic.
- A metric with a multi-year trend.
- A counterexample.

Examples: retention after price increases, stable share without excess promotion, cost gap after normalizing mix, or density advantages visible per route/store.

### 4. Separate source from reinforcement

Identify the original moat source and the reinforcing loop. Market share may reinforce scale, but market share itself is not the source. Brand awareness may be an output, while trusted performance is the source.

### 5. Run the attacker simulation

Assume a well-funded, patient competitor can copy the visible product. Ask:

- What must it spend, wait for, or risk?
- Can customers multi-home or switch gradually?
- Does technology collapse the switching path?
- Does the incumbent's response destroy its own economics?

### 6. Test durability

Score the mechanism across:

- Reproduction time and cost.
- Customer incentive to stay.
- Rate of technological/regulatory change.
- Dependence on one channel, person, patent, or contract.
- Need for continual spending merely to stand still.

### 7. Map moat erosion

Name leading indicators before financial results fall: weaker cohorts, higher incentives, falling price realization, rising service costs, channel bypass, lower engagement quality, regulation, or employee/customer concentration.

### 8. Reach a moat verdict

- `None`: no demonstrated protection of excess economics.
- `Narrow`: real but limited by segment, geography, product, or time.
- `Wide`: multiple reinforcing mechanisms with strong behavioral evidence.
- `Shrinking`: historically strong, but attack/erosion indicators are active.
- `Unproven`: plausible narrative without sufficient evidence.

## Output contract

Produce a `Moat Map` table:

```text
Mechanism | Economic prize | Direct evidence | Counterevidence
Attacker cost/time | Reinforcement loop | Erosion indicator | Verdict
```

Finish with the single strongest moat, the single most credible attack, confidence, and a monitoring dashboard of 3–6 metrics.

## Stop and escalate

Stop when customer behavior or cost structure is unavailable and management claims cannot be cross-checked. Escalate technical substitution, regulation, or industry cost curves to a qualified reviewer.

## Failure modes

- “Strong brand” without price/retention evidence.
- “Network effect” where more users add congestion rather than value.
- Market share mistaken for protection.
- Patent life mistaken for post-expiry durability.
- Historic margins used without competitor and capital context.
- Calling high switching pain a moat when customers are actively building an exit.

## Safety and truthfulness

Date all market evidence. Distinguish direct observation from inference. Do not infer anticompetitive conduct or legal violations without evidence; route legal issues to counsel.

## Self-review

- What exact owner economics does each mechanism protect?
- What customer behavior proves it?
- Could a well-funded attacker reproduce it?
- What leading indicator would show erosion early?
- Did I separate a temporary lead from a durable moat?
