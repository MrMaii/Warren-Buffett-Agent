# Analyze Business Quality

## Operating stance

Judge quality by the cash a business can produce and redeploy over time without fragile assumptions. Growth is an input, not the verdict; growth that consumes too much capital or weakens unit economics may destroy owner value.

## Inputs

Require, where applicable:

- Five to ten years of income, balance-sheet, and cash-flow statements.
- Segment economics and major accounting-policy notes.
- Customer groups, purchase frequency, churn/retention, and pricing history.
- Unit economics and the working/fixed capital required for growth.
- Industry structure, substitute behavior, regulation, and cycle exposure.
- Management's reinvestment claims and actual capital-allocation record.

Use current primary sources for a live business and timestamp all figures.

## Method

### 1. Map the owner cash engine

Trace:

```text
Customer need -> purchase -> reported revenue -> operating profit
-> working/fixed capital required -> taxes -> distributable owner cash
```

Explain cash timing, not only margin percentages.

### 2. Test demand quality

Assess:

- Mission-critical vs discretionary demand.
- Repeat behavior and customer concentration.
- Volume response after price changes.
- Contractual recurrence vs economically recurring demand.
- Refund, credit, cancellation, or channel dependence.

### 3. Reconcile accounting quality

Check revenue recognition, capitalization, stock compensation, acquisition adjustments, reserves, working-capital movements, and one-offs. Flag profit that cannot become cash without continual financing.

### 4. Measure capital intensity

Separate:

- Maintenance capital needed to defend current economics.
- Growth capital that can produce new economics.
- Working-capital absorption.
- Acquired growth and the full purchase price paid.

### 5. Estimate incremental returns

Prefer incremental analysis over headline ROIC:

```text
Incremental return ≈ change in normalized after-tax operating earnings
                     / incremental operating capital required
```

Use multi-year ranges and explain denominator choices. A high historic return with a declining incremental return is a warning.

### 6. Test reinvestment runway

Ask how much cash can be reinvested, for how long, at what return, and what evidence shows the opportunity is not already saturated. Distinguish a great business with no reinvestment runway from a great compounder.

### 7. Test resilience

Stress volume, price, input costs, customer loss, regulation, technology substitution, and financing access. Identify which costs are truly variable and whether capital spending can be deferred without damage.

### 8. Integrate stewardship

Evaluate whether management converts business quality into per-share value. Good operations can be neutralized by overpriced acquisitions, dilution, leverage, or poor reinvestment.

### 9. Assign a quality verdict

Use one of:

- `Exceptional`: durable customer economics, high incremental returns, low fragility, honest stewardship.
- `Good`: attractive economics with bounded weaknesses or limited runway.
- `Average`: earns near opportunity cost after full capital needs.
- `Fragile`: profits depend on cycle, financing, concentration, or favorable accounting.
- `Unclear`: evidence is insufficient or the model is outside the circle.

Do not convert the verdict to a security recommendation without price and value analysis.

## Output contract

Produce a `Business Quality Dossier`:

1. Plain-language cash engine.
2. Demand and revenue quality.
3. Normalized economics and capital intensity.
4. Incremental return range and reinvestment runway.
5. Resilience and key failure mode.
6. Management effect on per-share value.
7. Quality verdict, confidence, and missing evidence.
8. Metrics to monitor for deterioration.

## Stop and escalate

Stop the quality verdict when segment data is unusable, maintenance capital cannot be bounded, customer economics depend on unavailable private data, or a technical/regulatory mechanism dominates. Escalate the narrow unresolved mechanism.

## Failure modes

- Equating revenue growth with quality.
- Using gross margin as a moat proxy.
- Ignoring working capital and acquired growth.
- Using historic ROIC without incremental returns.
- Treating recurring revenue labels as proof of recurring demand.
- Calling a business bad only because it is capital intensive; the question is return on required capital.

## Safety and truthfulness

Label adjustments and estimates. Do not fabricate unit economics. Do not infer customer retention from management adjectives. Company quality and investment attractiveness must remain separate.
